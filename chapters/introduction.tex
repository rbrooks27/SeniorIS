\section{Research Problem and Motivation}\label{sec:research-problem}
    Anthropogenic chemicals enter the environment through industrial activities, agricultural practices, and the manufacture and use of consumer products. These inputs include pharmaceuticals, pesticides, industrial chemicals, and plastic-associated compounds, among other substances. Some chemicals are sufficiently persistent or mobile to move beyond their immediate sources, complicating efforts to determine their distribution and environmental fate~\cite{Manz2023,Blais2015,Wilkinson2022}. Their occurrence is not confined to urban or industrial settings. Investigations of mountain regions and remote waters demonstrate that chemical pollution can be detected far from major emission centers, although the relative contributions of long-range transport and local activities differ among sites~\cite{Daly2005,Xu2011,Ma2025}.

    The analytical problem extends beyond determining whether a sample contains selected contaminants. Environmental samples may produce many signals in liquid chromatography--high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). These signals are processed into molecular features, which are characterized by measurements such as mass-to-charge ratio and retention time. However, detecting a feature does not establish the chemical identity of the substance responsible for it. Traditional targeted measurements are useful when the compounds of interest are known in advance; they are less suited to surveying chemical features that were not anticipated when a method was developed. Suspect screening and non-targeted analysis (NTA) broaden the range of features that can be investigated, yet incomplete reference libraries and uneven spectral coverage continue to constrain identification~\cite{Manz2023,Schulze2020,Hupatz2025}.

    This limitation is especially important for remote environmental samples. A database match may support a candidate assignment for one feature, while other features in the same dataset remain unannotated despite containing potentially useful tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) information. Molecular networking offers a way to examine these data by representing features as nodes and retaining relationships between features with sufficiently similar MS/MS spectra. The resulting network can help researchers explore candidate chemical relationships and prioritize features for further study~\cite{Wang2016,Nothias2020,Bittremieux2022}. However, a network connection is not itself a confirmed molecular identification~\cite{Schymanski2014}.

    The present Senior Independent Study addresses a second, connected problem: how to develop a dependable computational workflow that allows chemists to construct, inspect, preserve, and interpret molecular networks. Constructing these networks requires more than displaying nodes and edges. Software must correctly associate molecular features with their MS/MS spectra, calculate and filter spectral similarities using documented parameters, and preserve the underlying chemical metadata and computational settings. These requirements introduce challenges involving data structures, algorithms, software architecture, and reproducibility. For the College of Wooster's environmental HRMS research, this project therefore combines environmental chemistry with software engineering to develop and evaluate an interactive molecular-networking workflow. Environmental HRMS datasets provide both the motivating scientific application and the basis for chemical case studies.

        Once released, an organic chemical's distribution depends on its physicochemical properties, its susceptibility to degradation, and the environmental processes acting on it. Atmospheric transport is one route by which certain pollutants can reach distant ecosystems. Volatilization, emissions associated with combustion or other activities, and the movement of particle-associated compounds can introduce chemicals into the atmosphere. During transport, reactions such as oxidation and photolysis may alter the original substances, producing transformation products with properties that differ from those of their precursors~\cite{Daly2005,Zahn2024}. The presence of a chemical in a remote sample, however, does not by itself establish its source or the pathway by which it arrived.

        For some semivolatile organic compounds (SVOCs), repeated volatilization, atmospheric movement, and redeposition can contribute to long-distance redistribution. This sequence is often described as the \emph{*grasshopper effect*}. Global fractionation and related temperature-dependent partitioning processes can favor the transfer of certain chemicals toward cooler regions, including mountain environments~\cite{Wania1996,Daly2005}. These mechanisms are particularly relevant to persistent compounds whose transport and deposition can occur over extended timescales. They should not be generalized to every pharmaceutical, pesticide, or other chemical detected by HRMS, because environmental behavior varies substantially among substances.

        Mountain topography and climate influence where transported contaminants are deposited. Orographic precipitation can increase wet deposition as air masses rise and cool, while snowfall can scavenge some gas-phase and particle-associated chemicals. Temperature gradients also affect air--surface exchange and the partitioning of SVOCs among air, snow, vegetation, and other surfaces~\cite{Catalan2015,Herbert2006,Davidson2003}. These processes operate together, rather than as independent explanations for every observation at a mountain site.

        High-altitude regions offer valuable settings for examining contaminant transport because their climatic conditions can favor deposition, seasonal storage, and subsequent redistribution of chemicals. Mountain snow and ice may temporarily retain atmospheric contaminants, and melting can transfer some of these substances into downstream waters. The timing and magnitude of release depend on snowpack processes and the physicochemical properties of the chemicals involved~\cite{Herbert2006,Meyer2008}. These processes are important when interpreting surface-water samples because chemicals detected in water may reflect both recent inputs and substances previously deposited in snow or ice. However, detecting a chemical feature alone does not establish its ecological effects or the extent of exposure to organisms.

        Research on mountainous environments has documented persistent organic contaminants and other human-associated chemicals in regions geographically distant from major industrial centers~\cite{Daly2005,Xu2011,WangCong2019}. Studies of the Qinghai--Tibetan Plateau also demonstrate that contamination patterns can be influenced by human activity within a highland basin rather than solely by long-range atmospheric transport~\cite{Ma2025}. Consequently, remote environments should not automatically be considered free of local pollution sources. Tourism, settlements, transportation, and regional agricultural activities may contribute alongside atmospheric inputs, depending on the location and chemical involved. Distinguishing among these possible contributions requires additional environmental and analytical evidence.

Seasonal changes in temperature, snow cover, and precipitation can influence the balance between deposition, storage, and re-emission of semivolatile organic compounds~\cite{Daly2005,Herbert2006}. \section{LC-HRMS, Suspect Screening, and Non-Targeted Analysis}\label{sec:lc-hrms}

    \subsection{LC-HRMS and Tandem Mass Spectrometry}\label{subsec:lc-hrms-msms}

        % Liquid chromatography separates components of a sample before they enter a mass spectrometer. High-resolution mass spectrometry measures ions with high mass accuracy and provides information used to characterize molecular features. In tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), selected precursor ions are fragmented and the resulting product-ion spectra provide additional evidence about molecular structure. These data support chemical annotation and comparison, but their interpretation depends on data quality and available reference information.
        % TODO: Describe the actual instrument, chromatography, ionization modes, and
        % acquisition conditions in Chapter 2 after checking laboratory records.

       Liquid chromatography--high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) combines chemical separation with accurate mass measurements to investigate complex mixtures. In liquid chromatography (LC), a liquid mobile phase carries sample components through a column containing a stationary phase. Because compounds interact differently with these phases, they move through the column at different rates and elute at different times. The time at which a compound elutes is known as its retention time. This separation helps reduce interference among compounds in complex environmental samples, although some substances may still elute together. Following separation, compounds are converted into gas-phase ions through an ionization process such as electrospray ionization (ESI), allowing them to be detected by the mass spectrometer~\cite{Manz2023,Kind2018}.

        High-resolution mass spectrometry measures the mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$) of ions with high resolving power and mass accuracy. Resolving power describes the instrument's ability to distinguish ions with closely spaced $m/z$ values, while mass accuracy describes how closely a measured value agrees with the expected value. During full-scan mass spectrometry (MS1), the instrument records ions over a selected mass range, providing information about the chemical components present in a sample. Computational processing of these measurements can identify molecular features characterized by properties such as $m/z$, retention time, signal intensity, and isotope patterns. Accurate mass measurements can help constrain possible elemental compositions, but they generally do not establish a unique molecular structure~\cite{Kind2018,Manz2023}.

        Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) provides additional chemical information by examining how selected ions fragment. An ion chosen for fragmentation is called a precursor ion. During collision-induced dissociation, the precursor ion collides with gas molecules and may break into smaller product ions. The resulting MS/MS spectrum records the $m/z$ values and relative intensities of these fragments. Because molecular structures influence fragmentation behavior, these spectra can provide evidence about functional groups, molecular substructures, and possible chemical identities. Comparing experimental MS/MS spectra with reference spectra can support candidate annotations, while similarities between spectra can also help investigate relationships among features that lack direct library matches~\cite{Kind2018,Hupatz2025}.

        The usefulness of LC-HRMS/MS therefore extends beyond detecting chemical signals. Combining chromatographic separation, accurate mass measurement, and fragmentation information provides multiple forms of evidence for investigating compounds in complex environmental mixtures. However, interpreting these measurements requires computational processing to detect features, associate them with the appropriate MS/MS spectra, and retain the analytical information needed for comparison. Software such as MS-DIAL supports these processing tasks, providing a connection between experimental measurements and the computational analyses developed later in this thesis~\cite{Tsugawa2015}. The specific instrumentation, ionization modes, acquisition strategy, and processing parameters used for the environmental datasets are described in Chapter~2.

    \subsection{Molecular Features Versus Identified Compounds}\label{subsec:features-compounds}

        % A molecular feature is an observed signal characterized by analytical attributes such as mass-to-charge ratio, retention time, and, when available, isotope or MS/MS information. A feature should not automatically be treated as a confirmed compound. Adduct formation, in-source fragmentation, isomers, incomplete spectra, and limited reference information can complicate interpretation.
        % This thesis uses the term \emph{feature} for detected signals whose chemical identities have not been confirmed. The term \emph{compound} is reserved for cases where the evidence supports that terminology. Network connections and candidate annotations are evidence to investigate, not substitutes for appropriate chemical confirmation.

        A \emph{molecular feature} is an analytical observation rather than a confirmed chemical identity. In LC-HRMS, features are generally characterized by properties such as mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$), retention time, and signal intensity. However, a single compound can produce multiple observed signals through isotope patterns, adduct formation, or fragmentation. An adduct is an ion formed when a molecule becomes associated with another chemical species, such as a proton or sodium ion. In addition, different compounds can sometimes produce overlapping signals. For example, isomers have the same molecular formula but differ in the arrangement of their atoms, making them difficult to distinguish using accurate mass alone. Consequently, the number of detected features does not necessarily correspond to the number of unique chemical compounds in a sample. Likewise, a spectral library match or a relationship to an annotated feature does not automatically establish a confirmed chemical identity~\cite{Manz2023,Schymanski2014}.

        Confidence in a chemical assignment must therefore reflect the available analytical evidence. Schymanski and colleagues proposed a five-level framework for communicating confidence in HRMS-based small-molecule identification, ranging from confirmed structures at Level 1 to exact-mass evidence at Level 5. Intermediate levels represent varying degrees of structural or molecular-formula information, depending on the available evidence~\cite{Schymanski2014}. Within this thesis, the term \emph{feature} refers to a detected analytical signal without implying that its chemical identity has been established. A \emph{candidate annotation} or \emph{tentative identification} indicates that an assignment is supported by some analytical evidence but remains uncertain. Confirmed identification requires stronger evidence, such as appropriate comparison with an authentic reference standard. This distinction is particularly important in molecular networking, where connections between features may suggest chemical relationships without proving their molecular identities. The confidence associated with individual case-study features will be reported according to the supporting evidence rather than inferred from network appearance.

    \subsection{Suspect Screening and Non-Targeted Analysis}\label{subsec:suspect-nta}

        % Targeted analysis focuses on a predefined set of compounds. Suspect screening examines data for compounds on a suspect list, including compounds that may not be part of a conventional targeted method. Non-targeted analysis (NTA) aims to detect and investigate a broader range of chemical features without restricting the analysis to a fixed list of target compounds.
        % These approaches are complementary. Suspect screening can investigate compounds for which there is a prior reason to look, while NTA can retain information about features not anticipated in advance. Neither approach guarantees that every detected feature can be identified. Candidate assignments must be evaluated against available mass, retention-time, isotope, and fragmentation evidence, as applicable.

        Environmental chemical analysis can be approached through targeted analysis, suspect screening, and non-targeted analysis (NTA). Targeted analysis focuses on a predefined group of compounds, often using established analytical methods and reference standards to support identification and quantification. For example, a targeted method might measure a selected group of pesticides known to occur in environmental waters. Suspect screening expands this approach by examining acquired data for compounds on a broader list of chemicals of interest, even when authentic reference standards are not available for every candidate. NTA takes a wider approach by detecting and investigating chemical features without requiring that all potential compounds be specified before analysis~\cite{Manz2023,Schulze2020}.

        These strategies are complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Suspect screening can prioritize chemicals associated with known environmental concerns, while NTA allows researchers to investigate additional features that were not anticipated during method development. Published investigations of pesticides in rain and snow, as well as broader organic chemical mixtures in snowfall, demonstrate the value of these approaches for environmental LC-HRMS research~\cite{Gray2022,NinoSuastegui2025}. However, expanding the number of detectable features also increases the challenge of interpreting the resulting data. Researchers must determine which signals represent meaningful chemical observations, evaluate possible identities using available evidence, and prioritize features that require additional investigation.

        These approaches also contribute to broader efforts to characterize the \emph{exposome}, which encompasses environmental and other exposures experienced over time~\cite{Manz2023}. Although suspect screening and NTA can provide information about portions of this chemical landscape, a single analytical dataset cannot characterize the entire exposome. The present project focuses more specifically on examining chemical features and their spectral relationships in existing high-altitude environmental datasets, rather than measuring total exposure or evaluating human health effects.

    \subsection{Limitations of Current Identification Approaches}\label{subsec:identification-limitations}

        % Reference databases and spectral libraries are incomplete. A detected feature may not have a suitable reference spectrum, and different structures can sometimes produce similar or partially overlapping fragmentation patterns. Data quality, acquisition conditions, and processing choices can also affect the evidence available for interpretation.

        % Identifying and prioritizing unknown features therefore requires researchers to combine different types of evidence and preserve uncertainty in their conclusions. A computational tool can help organize data and make relationships easier to inspect, but it should not convert a similarity score or network connection into an unsupported claim of chemical identity.
        Despite advances in high-resolution mass spectrometry, many detected features remain without confident chemical annotations. Reference databases may not contain a relevant compound or its experimental MS/MS spectrum, while differences in acquisition conditions can complicate comparisons with available references. Additionally, incomplete fragmentation data, overlapping signals, and similarities among chemical structures can make it difficult to distinguish competing candidate identities. Reviews of environmental NTA and computational structural annotation emphasize these limitations, as well as the importance of data quality, appropriate analytical evidence, and careful reporting of identification confidence~\cite{Schulze2020,Hupatz2025,Fisher2022}.

        These challenges create a gap between \emph{detection} and \emph{interpretation}. Increasing the number of observed features does not necessarily increase the number of confidently identified compounds. Computational approaches can help organize analytical evidence, identify promising spectral comparisons, and prioritize features for additional investigation. However, these methods must preserve uncertainty rather than treating every computational relationship as a confirmed chemical conclusion.

        The limitations are not exclusively chemical. The computational workflow must also preserve the relationships between detected features, their associated MS/MS spectra, and the parameters used during data processing. If these connections are lost or handled inconsistently, useful chemical information may become difficult to recover, compare, or interpret. Reliable data organization, transparent algorithms, and reproducible processing are therefore important for supporting chemical investigations. Molecular networking offers one approach for examining relationships among detected features, and its scientific and computational foundations are developed in the following section.

\section{Molecular Networking and Theoretical Foundations}\label{sec:molecular-networking}

    \subsection{MS/MS Fragmentation and Spectral Similarity}\label{subsec:fragmentation-similarity}

        % MS/MS spectra represent product ions generated when a precursor ion fragments under specified conditions. Related molecules may share some fragments or fragmentation patterns, so comparing spectra can provide clues about structural similarities. The strength of these inferences depends on spectrum quality, the comparison method, and the chemical context.
        % Spectral similarity is best treated as a way to prioritize and explore possible relationships. It is not, on its own, definitive evidence that two features represent the same molecule or lie on a specific transformation pathway.

        Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) provides information about molecular structure by recording the product ions generated when a selected precursor ion undergoes fragmentation. The resulting spectrum consists of fragment mass-to-charge ($m/z$) values and their corresponding intensities. Because the bonds and structural arrangements within a molecule influence how it fragments, compounds with related structures may produce some of the same product ions or exhibit similar fragmentation patterns. These relationships provide the chemical foundation for comparing MS/MS spectra and investigating features that lack direct reference-library matches~\cite{Wang2016,Bittremieux2022}.

        From a computational perspective, an MS/MS spectrum can be represented as a collection of numerical pairs, where each pair contains a fragment $m/z$ value and its measured intensity. This representation allows a computer to compare spectra mathematically rather than relying exclusively on visual inspection. However, two spectra cannot be compared meaningfully by simply assuming that their peaks appear in the same order. The software must first determine which fragment peaks correspond to one another. This process, known as \emph{peak matching} or \emph{spectral alignment}, typically uses a mass tolerance to account for small differences between measured fragment masses. Tolerances may be expressed in daltons (Da) or parts per million (ppm), depending on the comparison method and instrument characteristics~\cite{Bittremieux2022}.

        Spectral preprocessing can further influence the quality of these comparisons. Experimental spectra may contain noise, low-intensity peaks, or signals that contribute little useful structural information. Computational procedures may therefore filter selected peaks, normalize intensities, or apply other transformations before calculating similarity. Intensity normalization places spectra on a comparable numerical scale, while filtering can reduce the influence of unwanted signals. These operations must be applied consistently because different preprocessing choices can change which peaks are matched and how strongly two spectra are considered related~\cite{Bittremieux2022,DallaValle2025}.

        Although structurally related compounds may share fragments, the relationship between molecular structure and spectral similarity is not one-to-one. Different compounds can generate overlapping product ions, and the same compound can produce different relative fragment intensities under different collision energies or instrument conditions. Some comparison methods account for possible structural differences by allowing fragment alignments shifted according to precursor-mass differences. Such approaches can reveal additional relationships beyond direct fragment matching, but they do not establish the chemical explanation for those differences~\cite{Bittremieux2022,Schmid2021}.

        Molecular networking uses these computational comparisons to organize spectral relationships across many features simultaneously. Instead of investigating each spectrum independently, researchers can examine groups of features whose fragmentation patterns satisfy defined similarity criteria. An annotated feature may provide useful context for neighboring features without direct library matches. These relationships can help prioritize candidate structures or chemical families for further investigation, provided that the underlying fragmentation evidence and identification uncertainty remain accessible~\cite{Wang2016,Nothias2020,Ernst2019}.

    \subsection{Cosine Similarity and Molecular-Network Construction}\label{subsec:cosine-network}

        % A spectral similarity score quantifies the correspondence between two spectra under a specified comparison procedure. Cosine similarity is one common approach. After spectra are represented and peaks are matched according to defined rules, the score summarizes alignment between the resulting spectral vectors. The score depends on implementation choices such as peak matching, mass tolerances, preprocessing, and filtering.
        % In a molecular network, each node represents a molecular feature and an edge represents a relationship that meets the selected similarity criteria. Edge weights or labels can retain the similarity score so researchers can inspect the computational relationship. Thresholds affect the resulting graph: stricter criteria may remove weaker connections, while more permissive criteria may create a denser network. The chosen settings and their rationale should therefore be documented.

        Once MS/MS spectra have been represented numerically and their fragment peaks have been aligned, a similarity function can quantify the agreement between them. One commonly used approach is \emph{cosine similarity}, which measures the relationship between two vectors according to their orientation rather than their absolute magnitude. For spectral comparisons, the vectors describe fragment intensities using a common representation established through peak matching or alignment. The resulting similarity score summarizes how closely the represented fragmentation patterns agree under the chosen computational procedure~\cite{Wang2016,Bittremieux2022}.

        Let $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ represent two aligned spectral intensity vectors containing $p$ positions. The intensity values at position $i$ are denoted by $x_i$ and $y_i$, respectively. Their cosine similarity is calculated as

        \begin{equation}
            \operatorname{cos}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
            = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p} x_i y_i}
            {\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_i^2}\,
            \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{p}y_i^2}}.
            \label{eq:spectral-cosine}
        \end{equation}

        For nonnegative intensity vectors with nonzero magnitudes, cosine similarity ranges from zero to one. A value approaching one indicates stronger agreement between the represented spectra, while a value approaching zero indicates little agreement under the selected alignment. The numerator measures the combined contribution of corresponding intensities, while the denominator normalizes the result using the magnitudes of the two vectors. This normalization allows spectra with different overall signal intensities to be compared, although it does not eliminate differences caused by spectral quality, acquisition conditions, or preprocessing.

        An important distinction is that spectral cosine similarity depends on how the corresponding intensity positions are established. Before applying Equation~\ref{eq:spectral-cosine}, a comparison algorithm must decide which fragment peaks match within the specified mass tolerance and how unmatched peaks are represented. Implementations may also apply intensity weighting, spectral filtering, or minimum matched-peak requirements. Modified-cosine approaches can permit certain shifted fragment alignments based on precursor-mass differences, allowing comparisons that consider both shared fragments and potentially related fragmentation patterns. Consequently, two workflows using the term \emph{cosine similarity} may produce different scores if their preprocessing and matching procedures differ~\cite{Bittremieux2022,DallaValle2025}.

        Calculating a similarity score does not automatically create a molecular-network connection. A network-construction algorithm applies additional criteria to determine whether two features should be connected. For example, a workflow may require a minimum cosine score and a minimum number of matched fragments before retaining an edge. Other procedures can further restrict connections by keeping only sufficiently high-ranking neighbors or limiting the size of connected groups. Established GNPS workflows use combinations of these filtering approaches to organize spectral relationships~\cite{Wang2016,Nothias2020}. Because the filters determine which edges remain, different parameter choices can produce different network structures from the same underlying spectra.

        The number of possible comparisons also introduces an important computational challenge. If a dataset contains $n$ spectra and every unique pair is compared once, the number of pairwise comparisons is

        \begin{equation}
            N_{\mathrm{comparisons}} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}.
            \label{eq:pairwise-comparisons}
        \end{equation}

        This corresponds to quadratic, or $O(n^2)$, growth in the number of comparisons. For example, 1,000 spectra require 499,500 unique pairwise comparisons, while 10,000 spectra require 49,995,000 comparisons. The actual computational time also depends on the number of fragment peaks, the matching algorithm, and any preprocessing or candidate-selection methods. Nevertheless, this growth illustrates why computational efficiency becomes increasingly important when analyzing large LC-HRMS datasets. Strategies that reduce unnecessary comparisons or efficiently organize candidate relationships may therefore influence the scalability of a molecular-networking workflow.

LC-HRMS data are first processed to detect features and obtain associated MS/MS spectra. Spectral comparison methods then quantify relationships between those features, and graph-construction rules determine which relationships are retained. The resulting network provides a computational representation that can be examined through visualization and graph-analysis methods.

A reproducible implementation must specify its spectral representation, peak-matching procedure, similarity calculation, and edge-selection rules. The particular algorithms, parameters, and validation procedures used in the Wooster software will be documented in Chapter~2.

    \subsection{Graph Theory: Nodes, Edges, and Connected Components}\label{subsec:graph-theory}

        % Graph theory provides a formal way to represent relationships among objects. In molecular networking, nodes represent features and edges represent the spectral relationships retained by the workflow. A connected component is a set of nodes connected through paths in the graph. Isolated nodes have no retained edges under the selected network criteria.
        % These structures help summarize and navigate relationships in a dataset. However, a connected component should not automatically be interpreted as one chemical family, and an edge should not be treated as proof of shared identity or a reaction pathway. Interpretation requires considering the graph alongside spectral evidence, metadata, annotations, and analytical limitations.

        Graph theory provides a mathematical framework for representing objects and the relationships between them. A graph is commonly written as $G=(V,E)$, where $V$ is a set of vertices, or \emph{nodes}, and $E$ is a set of edges connecting selected pairs of vertices. In molecular networking, nodes can represent detected molecular features, while edges represent spectral relationships that satisfy the chosen computational criteria. Each node may also contain analytical information, such as precursor $m/z$, retention time, ionization polarity, or a candidate chemical annotation. Similarly, an edge may store a numerical similarity score and supporting information about how the relationship was calculated~\cite{Newman2003,Barabasi2016,Wang2016}.

        When the relationship between two features is based on a symmetric spectral-similarity calculation, the resulting network can be represented as an \emph{undirected graph}. In an undirected graph, a connection between features $u$ and $v$ does not imply a direction from one feature to the other. A \emph{weighted graph} additionally associates numerical values with its edges. For molecular networking, these weights may represent spectral similarity scores, although other relationship types require their own definitions. An edge weight describes the computed relationship under the selected method rather than the certainty of a chemical identification.

        Computer programs must store graph relationships in structures that support efficient access and analysis. One common representation is an \emph{adjacency list}, which maintains the neighboring vertices associated with each node. For example, if feature A is connected to features B and C, the adjacency representation records B and C as neighbors of A. An adjacency matrix provides another representation by storing relationships in a two-dimensional structure indexed by vertex pairs. These approaches involve different storage and access trade-offs. Adjacency lists are particularly useful for sparse graphs, where the number of retained edges is small relative to the number of possible connections. In contrast, an adjacency matrix stores a position for every possible vertex pair, requiring space proportional to the square of the number of vertices~\cite{Newman2003,Barabasi2016}.

        Graph algorithms allow researchers to examine relationships that may be difficult to interpret from a network image alone. A \emph{connected component} is a maximal group of vertices in which every pair is connected by some path through the graph. An \emph{isolated vertex} has no retained edges, while the \emph{degree} of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it in an undirected graph. These measures help describe network organization, identify disconnected features, and locate groups that may be useful for further chemical investigation. However, a connected component is not automatically a chemical family. Two features can belong to the same component through a sequence of intermediate connections without being directly similar to each other.

        Connected components can be identified through graph-traversal algorithms such as depth-first search (DFS) or breadth-first search (BFS). Both approaches systematically explore the vertices reachable from a starting node. DFS follows a sequence of connections before returning to explore alternatives, while BFS explores neighboring vertices in successive layers. When implemented using an adjacency-list representation, a complete traversal can run in $O(|V|+|E|)$ time, where $|V|$ is the number of vertices and $|E|$ is the number of retained edges. This efficiency is useful because the resulting graph may contain substantially fewer edges than the number of spectral pairs initially considered.

        Graph-analysis libraries provide reusable implementations of these data structures and algorithms. NetworkX, an open-source Python library, supports attributed graphs, weighted edges, connected-component analysis, graph traversal, and several graph-export formats~\cite{Hagberg2008}. Such functionality makes it a potential component of the Wooster molecular-networking software. However, NetworkX is a general-purpose graph library rather than a mass-spectrometry analysis system. It does not independently determine which fragment peaks match or whether two spectra are chemically similar. Those relationships must be calculated and validated through separate spectral-processing procedures before they can be represented as graph edges.

        Figure~\ref{fig:PrimaryMolecularNetworkExample} illustrates a conceptual molecular network with multiple connected features. The visual representation makes it possible to observe individual connections and groups within the network. Nevertheless, the scientific meaning of those relationships depends on the spectra, annotations, and computational criteria used to generate them.

They also establish an important separation between chemical evidence and computational representation: spectral comparisons determine which relationships are supported, while graph data structures and algorithms make those relationships possible to store, analyze, and explore.

    \subsection{Network Visualization and Interpretation}\label{subsec:network-visualization}

        % Network visualization can help researchers inspect complex relationships that may be difficult to explore in a table alone. Selecting nodes and edges, viewing feature metadata, examining similarity values, and filtering the network can connect the overall graph to the underlying chemical evidence. Visual encodings should communicate meaningful properties without implying more certainty than the data support.
        % The interface is most useful when users can move between the network-level view and the evidence behind a connection. Users should be able to inspect which features are connected, why an edge is present under the selected criteria, and what information is available about the features. Important settings and data provenance should be accessible enough to support repeatable analysis.

        Network visualization provides a way to examine relationships that may be difficult to recognize from tables of spectral similarities alone. Once a molecular network has been constructed, a visualization system assigns positions and visual properties to its nodes and edges. Features can be represented as circles or other symbols, while connecting lines indicate retained relationships. Color, node size, edge thickness, and labels can communicate additional information, such as annotation status, feature intensity, or spectral similarity. However, these visual properties must have clearly defined meanings so that the interface does not communicate more chemical certainty than the underlying evidence supports.

        Graph-layout algorithms determine how vertices are positioned within a visualization. A commonly used approach is a \emph{force-directed layout}, which models graph elements using simulated attractive and repulsive forces to produce a readable arrangement. Connected vertices tend to be drawn together, while repulsive forces help reduce overlap. ForceAtlas2 is one example of a force-directed layout method designed for network visualization~\cite{Jacomy2014}. These algorithms can make connected groups easier to recognize, but the resulting positions are mathematical display choices rather than experimental measurements. In particular, the physical distance between two nodes on a screen should not automatically be interpreted as a quantitative measure of chemical similarity.

        Interactive visualization extends the usefulness of a network by allowing researchers to investigate individual features and relationships. Rather than viewing a static diagram, a user can select a node, examine its neighbors, inspect an associated MS/MS spectrum, or retrieve available information about its precursor mass, retention time, and candidate annotations. Selecting an edge can similarly provide access to its similarity score, matched-fragment evidence, and computational settings. Research on interactive graph exploration emphasizes the importance of connecting visual overviews with operations that support users' analytical tasks~\cite{Pienta2015}.

        From a software engineering perspective, this functionality requires coordination between several components. A computational layer stores the graph structure and associated metadata, a visualization layer converts that information into graphical elements, and an interaction layer responds to researcher actions. These responsibilities can be separated so that changing the visual appearance of a network does not unintentionally change the underlying scientific calculations. For example, hiding weak edges in a browser may change the displayed network without recalculating spectral similarity. The software must distinguish such visual filtering from generating a new graph under different computational thresholds.

        Another important consideration is \emph{data provenance}, which refers to information describing the origin of data and the processes used to produce them. In molecular networking, provenance can include the original feature identifiers, associated spectra, software versions, preprocessing settings, similarity algorithms, and edge-selection parameters. Preserving this information allows researchers to understand why a connection exists and, where the necessary inputs are available, attempt to reproduce the analysis. Saving only the graphical positions of nodes and edges is insufficient because a network image does not contain all the analytical evidence or computational decisions used to construct it.

        These principles are relevant to the chemist-centered software developed in this Senior Independent Study. A scientifically useful interface should connect the overall network view to the evidence supporting individual features and relationships. Researchers should be able to move between graph-level patterns and detailed chemical information without losing the context of the original analysis. The intended purpose is not simply to produce more attractive molecular networks, but to support transparent investigation, consistent interpretation, and reproducible computational workflows. The specific website architecture, interactions, and evaluation methods will be described in later chapters.

    \subsection{Limitations of Spectral Similarity as Evidence of Chemical Identity}\label{subsec:spectral-limitations}

        % A high spectral similarity score indicates agreement under the selected computational comparison; it does not independently establish molecular identity. Similar spectra can arise for different structures, and structurally related compounds can produce different spectra under different conditions. Missing or low-quality MS/MS data may also weaken or prevent meaningful comparisons.
        % The software developed in this project is intended to support exploration of evidence rather than replace expert chemical judgment or confirmatory experiments. Where reference standards or other appropriate confirmation are absent, the thesis will distinguish tentative annotations and possible relationships from confirmed identifications.

        Spectral similarity provides useful evidence for investigating relationships among molecular features, but it cannot independently confirm the identity of an unknown compound. Different structures may produce shared fragment ions, while structurally related compounds can generate different spectra depending on ionization conditions, collision energy, and other experimental factors. Isomeric compounds may also exhibit closely related fragmentation patterns, making confident structural discrimination difficult without additional evidence. Consequently, a high similarity score supports agreement between spectra under a particular comparison procedure rather than proving that two features represent the same molecule or a confirmed structural analogue~\cite{Bittremieux2022,Hupatz2025}.

        Computational processing introduces additional sources of uncertainty. Peak filtering, intensity normalization, fragment-matching tolerances, and the selected similarity function can all influence the resulting scores. Network-construction thresholds and filtering rules then determine which relationships are retained. A stricter threshold may remove weaker connections, while a more permissive threshold may produce a denser graph containing additional uncertain relationships. These choices can alter connected components and the apparent organization of a network without changing the original experimental measurements~\cite{Wang2016,Nothias2020,DallaValle2025}.

        Data integrity is another essential limitation. A molecular-networking algorithm depends on correctly associating each feature with its corresponding MS/MS spectrum and analytical metadata. Incorrect identifiers, missing spectra, or inconsistently processed data can generate misleading relationships or prevent meaningful comparisons. Furthermore, the absence of an edge does not establish that two compounds are chemically unrelated. It may instead reflect insufficient fragmentation evidence, low spectral quality, or the exclusion of a potential relationship by the selected computational criteria.

        Chemical interpretation also requires care when examining precursor-mass differences or groups of connected features. A mass difference between two nodes may be consistent with a possible chemical modification, but it does not establish that one compound is a transformation product of another. Likewise, membership in a connected component does not prove a common environmental source or reaction pathway. These interpretations require supporting chemical evidence, and candidate annotations should retain confidence levels appropriate to the available information~\cite{Schymanski2014,Ernst2019,Chao2025}.

        These limitations emphasize the difference between computational reproducibility and chemical identification confidence. A workflow may consistently reproduce the same similarity scores and network structure while still containing uncertain chemical assignments. Conversely, useful experimental evidence may be difficult to interpret when the computational workflow does not preserve its source data and processing decisions. Both problems must therefore be addressed when developing software for chemical research.

        The molecular-networking workflow proposed in this Senior Independent Study is intended to support evidence-based investigation rather than automatic structural confirmation. By preserving spectral information, documenting computational parameters, and making individual relationships accessible for examination, the software aims to help chemists prioritize unknown features while maintaining appropriate scientific uncertainty. This combination of chemical interpretation and transparent computation provides the foundation for the software requirements and research objectives discussed in the following sections.

       Interpreting LC-HRMS/MS data requires multiple computational stages, each addressing a different analytical or mathematical problem. Raw mass spectrometry measurements must first be processed into identifiable features and associated fragmentation spectra. These observations can then be compared to investigate possible chemical relationships, and the resulting information must be organized into a form that researchers can examine. At the College of Wooster, MS-DIAL and the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking platform (GNPS) have provided important parts of this process. Although both programs support chemical analysis, they differ in their underlying responsibilities, computational methods, and approaches to presenting results~\cite{Tsugawa2015,Wang2016,Nothias2020}.

        MS-DIAL, which stands for Mass Spectrometry--Data Independent Analysis, is an open-source application developed for processing mass spectrometry data. Despite its name, the software supports both data-independent acquisition (DIA) and data-dependent acquisition (DDA), among other analytical methods. The program was originally developed to address complex MS/MS data processing and has expanded to support feature detection, chromatographic alignment, chemical annotation, and other analytical operations~\cite{Tsugawa2015,Tsugawa2018,Tsugawa2020}. MS-DIAL 5 provides these capabilities through a desktop application built within the .NET software ecosystem, with separate computational and graphical components. This architecture allows established analytical processing methods to operate within an interface designed for mass spectrometry researchers.

        The MS-DIAL processing workflow begins with the import of experimental mass spectrometry data. Its feature-detection procedures examine analytical signals to locate chromatographic peaks and characterize them using measurements such as mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$), retention time, and signal intensity. When multiple samples or analytical runs are processed, feature alignment attempts to associate corresponding observations across datasets using criteria such as mass and retention-time agreement. Available MS/MS spectra can then be associated with detected features, allowing reference-based annotation and downstream spectral analysis. These procedures help transform large collections of instrumental measurements into organized analytical records that researchers can interpret~\cite{Tsugawa2015,Tsugawa2018}.

        An important aspect of MS-DIAL is its ability to export processed information for use in other software. MS-DIAL 5 documentation describes exports containing peak lists, alignment results, representative MS/MS spectra, and processing parameters. Spectral information can be exported in formats such as Mascot Generic Format (MGF) and Mass Spectral Library (MSP) format, while feature-level measurements can be represented in tabular files. MS-DIAL 5 also includes molecular-spectrum-networking functionality, meaning that network construction is not exclusive to GNPS. However, the availability of these capabilities does not automatically establish that every exported feature, spectrum, annotation, and parameter can be transferred into another application without additional validation. The particular export structures used in this project must be examined to determine how their records correspond.

        GNPS provides a complementary computational environment for mass spectrometry analysis. Developed as a community-based platform, GNPS supports spectral-library searching, molecular networking, data sharing, and collaborative interpretation of MS/MS information~\cite{Wang2016}. Unlike software that primarily processes raw chromatographic data, GNPS places substantial emphasis on comparing fragmentation spectra and organizing their relationships. One established approach is classical molecular networking, in which submitted MS/MS spectra are organized through spectral comparisons and may be grouped into consensus representations. A second approach, feature-based molecular networking (FBMN), incorporates chromatographic features identified by upstream software and maintains relationships between those features and their associated spectra~\cite{Nothias2020}.

        Feature-based molecular networking is especially relevant to the Wooster research workflow because it connects existing LC-HRMS processing with spectral-network analysis. In a typical MS-DIAL-to-GNPS workflow, MS-DIAL first produces a feature quantification table and a corresponding file of representative MS/MS spectra. The quantification table records feature-level information and measurements across samples, while the spectral file contains fragmentation information associated with those features. GNPS then uses these inputs to compare spectra, calculate similarity scores, apply networking criteria, and generate relationships that can be explored alongside available annotations and sample information~\cite{Nothias2020}. Correctly matching feature identifiers between these inputs is essential because a network connection is scientifically useful only when it can be traced to the appropriate analytical observations.

        Constructing a GNPS molecular network involves more than calculating a single similarity score. Its workflows can apply fragment-mass tolerances, minimum matched-peak requirements, spectral-similarity thresholds, and additional filtering rules that determine which relationships remain in the graph. These decisions influence network density, connected components, and the relationships available for chemical interpretation. GNPS also provides access to reference spectral libraries that can support candidate annotations, although a library match does not independently guarantee confirmed chemical identity~\cite{Wang2016,Nothias2020}. Because these operations depend on specific computational settings, interpreting or reproducing a GNPS network requires more information than its final graphical appearance.

        GNPS2 represents a newer, separately documented platform with dedicated workflows for feature-based molecular networking and other mass spectrometry analyses. Its FBMN workflow supports inputs such as feature quantification tables and representative MGF or MSP spectral files, together with optional sample metadata. GNPS2 therefore remains an important existing alternative when evaluating the need for a locally developed application. However, GNPS and GNPS2 should not be assumed to use identical interfaces, parameter defaults, or computational behavior. Meaningful comparisons require identifying the particular workflow, software version, input files, and settings used for each analysis.

The figure emphasizes the movement from processed LC-HRMS/MS data to molecular-network relationships rather than depicting the final architecture of the proposed Wooster website.

        A further computational consideration is how the resulting molecular network should be represented and analyzed within a new software system. NetworkX is an open-source Python library that provides data structures and algorithms for constructing, modifying, and investigating graphs~\cite{Hagberg2008}. It supports representations such as undirected graphs, weighted edges, and nodes containing additional attributes. Within a molecular-networking application, these capabilities could be used to store feature identifiers, spectral-similarity scores, chemical metadata, and graph relationships. NetworkX also supplies algorithms for examining neighbors, connected components, and other structural properties. However, it does not independently process LC-HRMS measurements, align MS/MS fragments, or determine whether two spectra are chemically related. MS-DIAL can remain responsible for established upstream analytical processing, while existing GNPS workflows provide scientific methods and results against which a local implementation can be evaluated. The Wooster website would then connect these computational operations to an interface designed for local researchers. MS-DIAL 5 and GNPS2 already provide substantial data-processing and molecular-networking functionality. The existence of newer software versions does not eliminate the need to evaluate how well those tools support the specific tasks performed by Wooster researchers. A workflow may be scientifically capable of generating a network while still requiring researchers to move among multiple programs, manage several output files, or reconstruct the settings used for a previous analysis. Such challenges concern how computational tools are integrated into the laboratory's research practices rather than whether the individual programs can perform their intended calculations.

        One important technical challenge occurs when analytical information moves between software systems. A processed LC-HRMS feature may be represented by an identifier in an alignment table, while its associated MS/MS spectrum appears in another file. If these identifiers are inconsistent, missing, or incorrectly interpreted, an imported network may connect the wrong analytical records or omit information needed for chemical interpretation. Differences in file formats, retention-time units, intensity definitions, and missing-value conventions can introduce similar problems. These risks are particularly relevant when integrating software developed independently, because compatible file extensions do not necessarily guarantee compatible data structures or field meanings.

        From a computer science perspective, these problems involve \emph{data integrity}, which concerns the accuracy, consistency, and preservation of information as it is processed and transferred. For molecular networking, data integrity requires maintaining valid relationships among feature identifiers, associated spectra, candidate annotations, and their source records. A scientifically meaningful interface should not silently discard unmatched records or present a network relationship without sufficient information about its origin. Instead, the computational workflow should define how inputs are checked, how invalid or incomplete information is handled, and how the resulting graph can be traced back to its source data.

        A related requirement is \emph{reproducibility}, or the ability to repeat a computational analysis using documented inputs, processing methods, and settings. A molecular network can change when fragment-matching tolerances, similarity thresholds, or filtering rules change, even when the underlying experimental data remain unchanged. Preserving only the final visualization is therefore insufficient. Researchers also need access to the input files, relevant software versions, parameter values, and outputs used to construct the network. Established scientific-computing practices emphasize organized data, documented computational procedures, version control, and repeatable analyses~\cite{Wilson2017}.

        These concerns suggest that software quality should be evaluated through several related properties. \emph{Correctness} concerns whether the implemented calculations and data relationships behave as specified. \emph{Reliability} concerns whether the application performs its expected operations consistently and handles failures appropriately. \emph{Reproducibility} concerns whether analyses can be reconstructed under documented conditions. Finally, \emph{usability} concerns whether researchers can perform the necessary analytical tasks and understand the information presented to them. These properties are related but distinct: an application can calculate correct similarity scores while still being difficult to use, and a visually accessible network can remain scientifically incomplete if its supporting evidence cannot be retrieved.

        For the present study, these concerns will be translated into explicit requirements and evaluation procedures rather than treated as problems already solved by the proposed software. Relevant tests include verifying feature-to-spectrum associations, checking the preservation of chemical metadata, comparing graph results under controlled settings, and determining whether saved analyses can be reopened and exported. Reported local difficulties will be distinguished from limitations reproduced through testing. This approach allows the research to evaluate the new application according to observable behavior rather than assumptions about existing software.

MS-DIAL, GNPS, and GNPS2 provide extensive analytical and computational capabilities, but their interfaces and workflows are intended to support a broad range of researchers and experimental applications. The requirements of the College of Wooster's environmental HRMS research may differ from those of other laboratories in how data are organized, how chemical evidence is examined, and how researchers prefer to move between analytical results.         This approach is related to \emph{user-centered software design}, in which the needs, activities, and expectations of intended users guide system requirements and interface decisions. Rather than beginning with a list of software features and assuming they will be useful, the development process considers the questions users need to answer and the operations required to answer them. Research on end-user software engineering and interactive graph exploration provides a foundation for understanding how software functionality and interaction design influence users' ability to work with complex computational information~\cite{Ko2011,Pienta2015}. In this project, those principles are applied to chemists who need to investigate molecular features and spectral relationships without losing access to the underlying analytical evidence.

        For example, a Wooster researcher investigating an unannotated molecular feature may want to locate that feature in a network, examine its precursor $m/z$ and retention time, compare its MS/MS spectrum with those of neighboring features, and determine whether any nearby annotations provide useful chemical context. Each of these tasks requires connections between different kinds of information. A network visualization contains graph relationships, but the chemical evidence may be stored separately as spectra, feature tables, and annotation records. The proposed software must therefore coordinate these data sources so that researchers can move from a network-level overview to the evidence supporting an individual relationship.

        This requirement introduces a software architecture problem. The application must define how scientific data are imported and validated, how spectral relationships are calculated or obtained, how graph structures are represented, and how results are presented to users. A spectral-comparison component can evaluate fragmentation relationships using documented algorithms. Finally, a web interface can translate these computational results into interactive visualizations and chemical-information panels. Separating responsibilities can make the workflow easier to test, maintain, and modify without unnecessarily changing unrelated operations.

        An additional design objective is to support scientific traceability. A researcher selecting a network edge should be able to investigate more than its displayed similarity value. The application should provide access to the associated features, supporting spectral information, and relevant computational settings. Likewise, users should be able to preserve an analysis with enough information to understand how it was generated and recover its results for future investigation. These capabilities connect software engineering decisions directly to scientific interpretation, because the usefulness of a network depends on whether its underlying evidence remains available and understandable.

        The motivation for a Wooster-specific system is therefore not to reproduce every capability of MS-DIAL or GNPS2. The goal is to develop a smaller, focused workflow that reflects how Wooster chemists investigate environmental HRMS data. Existing software may already perform individual parts of these tasks, but the research question concerns whether their integration into a locally designed application can provide a more consistent and useful experience for the intended researchers. Decisions about which operations to implement independently and which existing capabilities to reuse must be supported by technical evaluation.

Repeated analyses can assess whether equivalent inputs and settings produce consistent results. Export and recovery tests can examine whether scientific information remains accessible after an analysis has been saved.         Ultimately, the proposed Wooster molecular-networking application is intended to function as a research tool designed with chemists' analytical practices in mind. Its computer science contribution lies in designing, implementing, and evaluating a computational workflow that connects data integrity, spectral comparison, graph analysis, interactive visualization, and reproducibility. The objective is not to claim that existing tools are scientifically inadequate, but to determine whether a locally tailored system can make molecular-networking analyses more accessible, transparent, and useful for the researchers who perform them.

    \subsection{Goal}\label{subsec:specific-aims-goal}
        The overarching goal of this project is to improve the interpretation of organic contaminants and other chemical features observed in surface-water samples from remote high-altitude environments by developing and evaluating a molecular-networking software workflow tailored to the College of Wooster's research needs. This hypothesis concerns the \emph{usefulness of spectral relationships and the software that exposes them}, not a presumption that most unknown features can be structurally identified from a network. The research therefore has two linked outcomes: a tested computational tool for constructing and investigating molecular networks, and evidence-based chemical case studies using the high-altitude datasets.

        For this study, reliability refers to the correct handling of analytical data, consistent execution of specified computational operations, and appropriate responses to incomplete or invalid inputs. Reproducibility refers to the ability to reconstruct analytical results using documented input data, software versions, and processing parameters. Usefulness concerns whether the workflow helps Wooster chemists investigate molecular features, examine spectral relationships, and access the evidence needed to interpret them.         The project combines analytical chemistry and computer science through five specific aims. The first two establish the experimental and chemical evidence available for investigation. The third and fourth focus on developing the computational workflow and interactive software, while the fifth evaluates the system's performance and its usefulness for environmental chemical research.
       
    \subsection{Aim 1: Characterize LC-HRMS/MS Data and Fragmentation Coverage}\label{subsec:aim-one}

       The first aim is to establish the analytical foundation required for molecular networking by characterizing the available liquid chromatography--high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS/MS) data from high-altitude surface-water samples. The research workflow uses reversed-phase C18 chromatography and electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive and negative modes to provide complementary views of compounds with different chromatographic and ionization behavior. The experimental methods and origins of the existing samples and data will be distinguished from the computational work performed for this thesis. The relevant instrument settings, analytical procedures, and processing methods will be documented in Chapter~2~\cite{Wenkai,Gray2022,NinoSuastegui2025}.

        Where the existing acquisition records permit, the project will examine the fragmentation coverage provided by iterative data-dependent acquisition (DDA). In this strategy, successive runs can use inclusion and exclusion lists to direct MS/MS acquisition toward additional precursor ions rather than repeatedly fragmenting the same abundant signals. Automated exclusion-list methods described in the lipidomics literature provide a methodological precedent, although their performance in that application should not be assumed to transfer unchanged to these environmental water samples~\cite{Koelmel2017}. The analysis will consider which detected features have associated MS/MS spectra, which features lack sufficient fragmentation evidence, and how these differences influence their suitability for molecular networking.

        Characterizing the available data also contributes to the computer science objectives of this project. Before implementing a molecular-networking workflow, it is necessary to understand how analytical features, fragmentation spectra, sample identifiers, and acquisition metadata are represented across the processed datasets. Different experimental runs may contain separate feature identifiers, while a single feature may have multiple associated spectra or no usable MS/MS spectrum. These relationships affect how the proposed software must organize, validate, and connect the imported information.

        The expected outcome of this aim is a traceable description of the processed datasets, their fragmentation coverage, and their suitability for downstream spectral comparisons. This characterization will establish the analytical requirements for the software's data model and import procedures without claiming exhaustive chemical detection or identification~\cite{Fisher2022,Schulze2020}.

    \subsection{Aim 2: Annotate Known Contaminants Using Existing Reference Resources}\label{subsec:aim-two}

        The second aim is to establish a chemical-annotation baseline using suspect screening, existing spectral libraries, and the analytical evidence available for each feature. The upstream workflow uses MS-DIAL for feature detection, spectral processing, alignment, and related analytical operations before candidate annotations and associated spectra are examined for molecular-networking analysis~\cite{Tsugawa2015,Tsugawa2020}. Suspect lists and reference resources can guide the investigation of environmental contaminants of concern, including pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and industrial chemicals. However, a match to a database entry must be distinguished from a confirmed identification~\cite{Manz2023,Gray2022,NinoSuastegui2025}.

        Candidate assignments will be evaluated using available evidence such as precursor accurate mass, isotope patterns, MS/MS fragmentation, retention behavior when informative, and comparison with appropriate reference spectra or standards~\cite{Kind2018,Hupatz2025}. Identification confidence will be communicated using the Schymanski framework, which distinguishes confirmed structures from tentative structural assignments and less specific molecular information~\cite{Schymanski2014}. Features without sufficient evidence for a confident annotation will remain explicitly unannotated rather than being assigned unsupported chemical identities.

        This aim also establishes how existing chemical information should be represented within the proposed software. Candidate names, reference-library information, precursor measurements, associated spectra, and identification confidence must remain connected to the correct analytical features. Preserving these relationships will allow the molecular-networking interface to distinguish annotated features from unannotated ones while retaining the evidence supporting each candidate assignment.

        The expected outcome is a documented collection of annotated features and their associated confidence levels. These annotations will provide a chemical starting point for interpreting molecular networks and a baseline for evaluating whether spectral relationships offer additional information beyond direct library matches~\cite{Chao2025}.

    \subsection{Aim 3: Develop a Molecular-Networking Workflow Beyond Direct Library Matches}\label{subsec:aim-three}

        The third aim is to design and implement the core computational workflow that compares MS/MS spectra and converts supported spectral relationships into a molecular network. Rather than relying exclusively on matches to known library entries, this approach investigates relationships among detected features, including those without confident annotations. Established molecular-networking methods demonstrate how spectral similarity can support the exploration of chemically related features and the prioritization of unknowns for further investigation~\cite{Wang2016,Nothias2020,Ernst2019}.

        A central challenge is developing a consistent computational representation of the analytical data. The software must accept defined outputs from the upstream processing workflow and correctly associate molecular features with their corresponding MS/MS spectra and chemical metadata. This requires establishing a data model that distinguishes analytical features, fragmentation spectra, annotations, and the relationships calculated between them. Input validation will be necessary to identify missing information, inconsistent feature identifiers, and other problems that could affect the interpretation of network results. Stable identifiers and traceable source records will help ensure that the software does not associate spectra with incorrect features.

        Once the analytical records have been organized, the workflow will apply documented spectral preprocessing and comparison procedures. These operations will include suitable peak filtering, fragment matching within specified mass tolerances, and cosine-based similarity calculations. The resulting scores will be evaluated using configurable criteria that determine whether a relationship should be retained as a network edge. Such decisions are important because spectral noise, peak alignment, similarity measures, and filtering thresholds can change the resulting network~\cite{Olivon2017,Bittremieux2022,DallaValle2025}. The selected methods and parameters will be documented so that the calculations can be examined and reproduced.

        The retained relationships will then be represented using graph data structures. Each node will correspond to a clearly defined molecular feature, while edges will represent spectral relationships that satisfy the selected computational criteria. Edge attributes can preserve similarity scores and supporting information, allowing researchers to distinguish the existence of a graph connection from the chemical evidence behind it. General-purpose graph-processing tools such as NetworkX provide methods for storing weighted graphs, examining neighboring nodes, and identifying connected components~\cite{Hagberg2008,Newman2003}. The suitability of these methods for the Wooster workflow will be evaluated as part of the software-development process.

        Computational efficiency is another consideration because the number of possible spectral comparisons increases rapidly with dataset size. Comparing every unique pair of $n$ spectra produces $n(n-1)/2$ candidate comparisons before filtering, creating quadratic growth in the number of comparisons. The implementation will therefore be evaluated with attention to processing time, data organization, and the practical size of networks generated from representative datasets. Performance improvements will be considered where measured limitations justify them, rather than assumed to be necessary before the workflow has been tested.

        The intended outcome is a reproducible molecular-network construction process that allows existing chemical annotations to provide context for unannotated features. The software will preserve the parameters and evidence used to generate spectral relationships so that researchers can investigate why individual connections exist. Shared fragments and precursor-mass differences may motivate hypotheses about possible structural relationships, but the computational workflow will not treat these associations as proof of molecular identity or environmental transformation pathways~\cite{Schymanski2014,Chao2025}.

    \subsection{Aim 4: Build an Interactive, Chemist-Centered Software Interface}\label{subsec:aim-four}

        The fourth aim is to develop an interactive website that allows chemists to construct, inspect, interpret, and preserve molecular-networking analyses without losing access to the underlying experimental evidence. While existing platforms such as MS-DIAL and GNPS2 provide substantial analytical and molecular-networking capabilities, the proposed application will focus on the particular tasks and preferences of researchers at the College of Wooster. The objective is not to reproduce the complete functionality of existing platforms, but to develop and evaluate a more focused workflow that supports how local chemists investigate environmental HRMS data.

        This approach is informed by user-centered software design, in which the requirements of intended users guide decisions about functionality and interaction. Understanding the questions chemists want to answer is important for determining which software features are necessary and how those features should be organized. Research on end-user software engineering and interactive graph exploration provides a foundation for designing tools around the activities of domain specialists rather than requiring users to adapt their research practices to an unfamiliar computational interface~\cite{Ko2011,Pienta2015}.

        For example, a researcher investigating an unannotated feature may want to locate that feature within a molecular network, examine its precursor $m/z$ and retention time, inspect neighboring features, and compare the fragmentation evidence supporting individual relationships. The interface must therefore connect the visual representation of the network to the analytical records from which it was constructed. Selecting a node should provide access to relevant feature information and available annotations, while selecting an edge should reveal the corresponding spectral relationship, similarity score, and supporting computational evidence.

        Supporting these interactions requires a software architecture that coordinates several responsibilities. A data-management component must organize imported feature records, spectra, and metadata. A computational component must perform or retrieve the spectral comparisons and construct the resulting graph. Graph-processing functionality must support the analysis and retrieval of relationships, while the browser interface must present these results in an understandable form. Separating these responsibilities can help make the software easier to test, maintain, and extend as the research requirements develop.

        The application will also distinguish between changes to the displayed network and changes to the underlying scientific analysis. For example, hiding selected edges in the interface may simplify visualization without changing their previously calculated similarity scores. In contrast, modifying spectral-matching parameters or network-construction thresholds may require generating a new analytical graph. Making these distinctions clear will help prevent users from confusing visualization choices with computational changes to the scientific results. Graph-layout methods will be selected to support readable exploration rather than treated as direct measurements of chemical similarity~\cite{Jacomy2014,Pienta2015}.

        Another primary objective is the preservation and recovery of analytical results. The proposed workflow will investigate how input data, network relationships, processing parameters, and associated chemical information can be saved and exported in a form that supports subsequent inspection. These capabilities are important because a reproducible research tool must preserve more than the visual appearance of a network. It must also retain sufficient information to explain how the analysis was generated and allow its results to be reconstructed under documented conditions~\cite{Wilson2017}.

        The expected outcome is a chemist-centered molecular-networking application that connects interactive visualization with analytical evidence, computational transparency, and reproducible data handling. Its design will prioritize the needs identified within the Wooster research workflow while retaining the flexibility to incorporate additional capabilities as those needs become better understood. The implemented interface and its effectiveness will be assessed through the software evaluation described in Aim~5.
        
    \subsection{Aim 5: Validate the Software and Apply It to Environmental Case Studies}\label{subsec:aim-five}

        The fifth aim is to evaluate whether the developed molecular-networking workflow is computationally reliable, reproducible, and useful for environmental chemical research. This evaluation will combine controlled software tests with representative analytical datasets and selected chemical case studies. The goal is to establish the strengths and limitations of the implemented system using documented evidence rather than assuming that its intended features will operate correctly or improve the research workflow.

        Computational correctness will be evaluated through tests of data import, feature identification, spectral comparisons, and graph construction. Controlled examples with known expected results will be used to verify calculations, relationships, and the handling of incomplete or invalid information. Tests will examine whether feature identifiers remain associated with the correct MS/MS spectra, whether similarity calculations follow the selected algorithms, and whether network edges satisfy the documented filtering criteria. Graph-level comparisons may include node counts, edge sets, edge weights, isolated features, and connected components. Where appropriate, selected results from established molecular-networking software will provide a comparison baseline, with differences interpreted in relation to preprocessing and algorithmic settings.

        Reproducibility will be evaluated by repeating analyses under equivalent input conditions and computational parameters. The resulting graph structures, similarity scores, metadata, and exported information will be compared to determine whether the software produces consistent analytical outputs. This evaluation will distinguish the underlying graph from its visual arrangement, since a graph-layout algorithm may place nodes differently without changing the scientifically relevant relationships. Tests of saving, reopening, and exporting analyses will also examine whether essential computational and chemical information can be recovered accurately~\cite{Wilson2017}.

        Performance evaluation will consider how the workflow behaves as the amount of analytical data increases. Measurements may include execution time, memory requirements, the number of processed spectra, and the size of the resulting networks. These observations will help determine whether the selected algorithms and data structures are suitable for the scale of the Wooster datasets. Research on similarity search and interactive graph exploration provides useful context for understanding computational and visualization constraints, although this project does not assume that every optimization described in the literature must be implemented~\cite{Bayardo2007,Pienta2015}.

        The usability evaluation will focus on whether the interface supports the analytical tasks for which it was designed. Representative activities may include locating an unannotated feature, examining its spectral neighbors, inspecting similarity evidence, reviewing candidate annotations, and recovering a previously saved analysis. Where feasible, feedback from Wooster researchers will be used to assess whether the application presents information clearly and supports their preferred workflow. This evaluation will consider scientific usefulness alongside software behavior, recognizing that an interface may perform its calculations correctly without necessarily meeting the needs of its intended users.

        The chemistry evaluation will use selected case studies from the high-altitude environmental datasets. One case study will focus on features with existing candidate annotations, while another will examine features that remain without confident library matches. These investigations will evaluate whether molecular networking reveals useful spectral relationships, provides context for candidate structures, or helps prioritize features for additional analysis. The interpretation will distinguish computational associations from confirmed chemical identities and report uncertainty using appropriate analytical evidence and identification-confidence principles~\cite{Fisher2022,Schymanski2014,Chao2025}.

        The expected outcome is a documented assessment of the proposed software's computational correctness, reliability, reproducibility, performance, and usability, together with evidence of its value and limitations in the selected environmental investigations. Success will not depend on identifying every unknown compound or demonstrating that the new website is universally superior to existing platforms. Instead, the evaluation will determine whether a Wooster-specific molecular-networking workflow can provide dependable computational results and meaningful support for the chemical questions motivating this Senior Independent Study.

\section{Project Scope and Thesis Contributions}\label{sec:scope-contributions}

    \subsection{Project Scope}\label{subsec:project-scope}

        This Senior Independent Study focuses on developing and evaluating a computational workflow for molecular networking using existing environmental LC-HRMS/MS data. The project includes examining the requirements of Wooster chemists, defining and validating data inputs, implementing spectral comparison and network-construction procedures, and developing an interactive website for investigating molecular features and their relationships. Selected environmental datasets will provide the basis for evaluating both the computational workflow and its usefulness for chemical interpretation.

        The proposed software is intended to complement established analytical tools rather than replace their complete functionality. MS-DIAL will continue to provide upstream mass spectrometry data processing, while existing molecular-networking platforms such as GNPS and GNPS2 provide important scientific methods and potential comparison baselines. The Wooster application will focus on the computational operations and user interactions most relevant to local researchers, including access to spectral evidence, preservation of feature metadata, reproducible network construction, and reliable saving and exporting of analyses. The specific software components, input formats, algorithms, and evaluation procedures will be described in Chapter~2.

        The project also has important scientific boundaries. Molecular networking cannot independently confirm the identity of an unknown compound, establish a chemical transformation pathway, or determine the environmental source of a detected feature. Likewise, software development does not establish chemical toxicity, ecological effects, or exposure levels. The chemical case studies will therefore distinguish experimental observations, candidate annotations, and computationally suggested relationships from conclusions supported by confirmatory chemical evidence.

    \subsection{Expected Computer Science Contributions}\label{subsec:cs-contributions}

        The primary Computer Science contribution is the design, implementation, and evaluation of a molecular-networking workflow and interactive website tailored to the research needs of College of Wooster chemists. This work involves translating scientific requirements into a software system that connects analytical data processing, spectral similarity calculations, graph representation, and interactive visualization. Particular attention will be given to the design of data structures, reliable associations between features and spectra, documented algorithms, and the organization of computational components.

        The research will also examine software reliability, reproducibility, and usability. Input validation and controlled algorithmic tests will assess computational correctness, while repeated analyses and export procedures will evaluate whether results and their supporting information can be preserved and reconstructed. User-centered design principles will guide the development of interactions that allow chemists to investigate network relationships and access relevant experimental evidence. Together, these efforts aim to establish a transparent and maintainable computational workflow rather than simply produce another molecular-network visualization.

        The significance of the Computer Science contribution will be evaluated through the implemented functionality, computational tests, performance measurements, and evidence of how effectively the software supports the intended research tasks. The resulting findings will also help identify the strengths, limitations, and potential future improvements of a locally developed scientific application.

    \subsection{Expected Chemistry Contributions}\label{subsec:chemistry-contributions}

        The primary Chemistry contribution is the application of molecular networking to existing environmental LC-HRMS/MS datasets from remote and high-altitude surface-water environments. Building on previous analytical research, the project will examine spectral relationships among features with existing candidate annotations and features that remain unannotated after reference-based screening. These investigations may provide additional chemical context for known contaminants and help prioritize unknown features for further study.

        Selected case studies will examine the available analytical evidence, including precursor masses, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, candidate annotations, and relationships within molecular networks. The analysis will consider whether connected features share informative spectral characteristics and whether these relationships support plausible hypotheses about chemical similarity. Identification confidence will be communicated using the Schymanski framework, and computational relationships will not be presented as confirmed molecular structures without appropriate evidence.

        The intended contribution is therefore not a comprehensive identification of every chemical detected in the environmental samples. Instead, the project aims to demonstrate how transparent molecular-networking methods can complement existing suspect-screening approaches, support chemical interpretation, and guide further investigation of complex environmental datasets.

    \subsection{Organization of the Thesis}\label{subsec:thesis-organization}

       The remainder of this thesis is organized into four chapters. Chapter~2 describes the research design, environmental datasets, analytical methods, software requirements, system architecture, spectral comparison algorithms, graph construction, and evaluation procedures. Chapter~3 presents the computational implementation and experimental results, including software validation, performance measurements, molecular-network characteristics, and selected environmental chemical case studies. Chapter~4 discusses the findings from both disciplines, evaluates the software's design decisions and limitations, and considers the implications for chemical interpretation and future computational research. Finally, Chapter~5 summarizes the principal contributions, addresses the central research question, and identifies opportunities for further development of the Wooster molecular-networking workflow.
